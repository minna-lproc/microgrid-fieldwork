How to use this instrument
The survey has two instruments. Use Instrument 1 (Parts A to H) with a sample of households and enterprises. Use Instrument 2 (Parts I to L) with barangay officials, facility heads and community leaders as key informants, one per community. Together they collect the inputs that microgrid design normally needs: a load profile, reliability and resilience expectations, willingness and ability to pay, the energy resources and land available, and the community’s acceptance of and role in the project. Before fieldwork, the instrument must pass an ethics review, be translated into the local language and be pilot-tested, and the team must follow the sampling and enumerator protocol below. Enumerators must not promise or imply that any project, tariff or level of service (including continuous 24-hour electricity) will be provided.
Sampling and enumerator protocol
Sampling frame. Before fieldwork, obtain the latest list of households per purok or sitio (from the barangay profile or population count). State the sampling frame, the household selection procedure (for example, random selection within each sitio) and the target sample size per sitio, and record these in the field notes.
Representation. Make sure remote sitios, low-income households, households with different livelihoods (farming, fishing, enterprises, wage work), renters and informal settlers, and other underserved groups are represented. Record every replacement of a selected household and the reason.
Enumerator protocol. Train enumerators on the consent script, the local-language wording and neutral questioning. Read questions as written, do not lead respondents, and do not make promises about the project.
Standardised conventions. Use the same recall periods, measurement units, response categories and skip logic throughout the instrument, and define them in the codebook.
Data quality. Prepare a data dictionary and codebook before field implementation. Where feasible, validate reported electricity use against appliance nameplates, electricity bills, generator records and actual measurements.

Instrument 2: Community Key Informants
Interview the barangay captain or a designated official, and separately the head of each community facility. Use records where available (barangay profile, school and health-station inventories, hazard maps). In barangays with ICC/IP communities, also interview the Indigenous Peoples Mandatory Representative (IPMR) as a key informant and, separately, the recognised customary leaders of the Indigenous Political Structure. The IPMR’s formal governance role is different from the customary authority of the Indigenous Political Structure and from the rights of affected community members.

Notes for the Project Team
Why each part is there
Part
Design input it provides
A, B
Baseline of customers, existing supply and current energy spending. Gives the fuel and cost savings against which a microgrid is judged. Households are selected under the sampling protocol so that remote sitios and underserved groups are represented.
C, D
Load profile by hour and season, plus expected growth, for sizing generation and storage. Sizing depends on both geographic conditions and load demand. Self-reported wattage and hours are validated against nameplates, bills, generator records and measurements where feasible. Essential, deferrable and discretionary loads are separated, suppressed demand and enterprise constraints are recorded, and productive uses are checked against solar availability.
E, J
Reliability and resilience targets, critical loads and hazards. The planning stage asks what level of uptime is needed, which operations must stay on, and which can tolerate instability. Availability, reliability and power quality are measured separately. Critical circuits are separated from total building load, and backup hours are set for emergencies.
F, G
Affordability, payment mode and customer-service design. Tariff, subsidy and service standards for unserved and underserved areas are determined through the regulatory process. Stated willingness to pay is not the same as ability to pay; it is analysed together with income range, seasonal income and affordability stress, and tested against lifeline, subsidy, prepaid and cross-subsidy scenarios. Survey answers are not a commitment to any tariff.
H, L
Social acceptance, governance, FPIC and local operation and maintenance capacity. These matter for the operator and for community consent. Governance covers formal and customary institutions (including IPMRs and Indigenous Political Structures), FPIC where legally required, ownership, benefit sharing, local employment, transparency, grievance handling and accountability, without assuming unpaid labour.
I, K
Community size, layout, access, land and natural resources, used for siting and network layout. Community-reported resources are verified by engineering studies across the seasons. Environmental sensitivity, cumulative impacts and end-of-life management of batteries, panels and electronic waste are assessed.
All parts
The framework is not judged on technical feasibility and affordability alone. Equitable access and distribution of benefits, meaningful participation of Indigenous Peoples and marginalised groups (with recognition of customary governance and ancestral domain rights), lifecycle financial sustainability, environmental safeguards, local ownership, capacity development, institutional accountability and long-term resilience are also considered.
